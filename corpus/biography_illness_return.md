# Srinivasa Ramanujan: Illness, Return to India, and Death (1917–1920)

## The Onset of Illness (1917)

Ramanujan's health had never been robust during his time in England, but by the spring of 1917, his condition had deteriorated to a critical point. He was admitted to a nursing home in Cambridge, and over the next two years he would cycle through several hospitals and sanatoria in England, including:

- Hill Grove Sanatorium, near Matlock, Derbyshire (1917)
- A nursing home in Wells, Norfolk
- The London hospital (Middlesex)
- Matlock House Sanatorium, Derbyshire (1917–1918)
- Colinette House, Putney (1918–1919)
- Fitzroy House, a sanatorium in London

At Matlock House, Ramanujan was deeply unhappy. The institution enforced strict regimens, and Ramanujan found the conditions oppressive. He reportedly attempted suicide at one point during this period — an incident in which he threw himself onto the tracks of the London Underground. Hardy rushed to intervene, and the incident was kept quiet to avoid legal consequences (attempted suicide was a criminal offense in England at the time). Some biographers, including Robert Kanigel in *The Man Who Knew Infinity*, have discussed this episode, though the exact details remain somewhat uncertain.

## Diagnosis and Medical Uncertainty

The precise nature of Ramanujan's illness has been a subject of medical debate for over a century. During his lifetime, the primary diagnosis was tuberculosis (TB), which was widespread and often fatal in that era. His symptoms — persistent cough, weight loss, fever, and general debility — were consistent with tuberculosis.

However, in 1994, Dr. D.A.B. Young published a detailed retrospective analysis in the journal *Notes and Records of the Royal Society* that proposed an alternative diagnosis: hepatic amoebiasis — a parasitic liver infection caused by *Entamoeba histolytica*. Young argued that Ramanujan may have contracted amoebic dysentery in India before traveling to England, and that the parasite could have caused a liver abscess that was misdiagnosed as tuberculosis. Key evidence for this alternative theory includes:

- Ramanujan had episodes of dysentery-like illness before coming to England.
- His symptoms did not always align perfectly with tuberculosis.
- He had a history of abdominal pain and liver-area discomfort.
- Tuberculosis treatment (such as it was at the time) did not produce significant improvement.
- Hepatic amoebiasis was poorly understood by English physicians at the time and would have been easily misdiagnosed.

The question remains unresolved, and it is possible that Ramanujan suffered from multiple conditions simultaneously. What is certain is that the English climate, wartime food shortages (particularly devastating for a strict vegetarian), and the stress of isolation and overwork all contributed to his physical decline.

## The Decision to Return to India

By late 1918, it was clear that Ramanujan was not recovering in England. Despite his recent elections as Fellow of the Royal Society and Fellow of Trinity College — honors that deeply gratified him — his health continued to worsen.

Hardy and others arranged for Ramanujan to return to India, hoping that the warmer climate, familiar food, and the presence of family would aid his recovery. Ramanujan departed from England on February 27, 1919, aboard the S.S. Nagoya. He arrived in Bombay (now Mumbai) on March 13, 1919, after a sea journey of about two weeks.

Ramanujan returned to a hero's welcome in India. The University of Madras had arranged a monthly allowance for him, and he was received with honor by the Indian mathematical community. However, his physical condition continued to deteriorate.

## Return to India and Continued Work (1919–1920)

After returning to India, Ramanujan went first to Madras and then to several locations in South India seeking treatment and rest. He stayed for a time in Kodumudi and later in Kumbakonam, his childhood home. He eventually settled in a rented house on Harrington Road in Chetput, Madras.

Despite his worsening health, Ramanujan continued to work on mathematics with an intensity that astonished and worried those around him. He worked propped up in bed, using his slate and notebooks, producing new results even as his body failed.

### Mock Theta Functions

The most significant mathematical work of Ramanujan's final months was his development of what he called "mock theta functions." These were described in his last letter to Hardy, written in January 1920, approximately three months before his death.

In this letter, Ramanujan introduced a new class of functions that he had discovered. He described 17 examples, which he organized by "order" — functions of order 3, 5, 7, and 10. He defined these functions by their behavior at certain points (cusps) of the upper half-plane, noting that they mimicked the behavior of ordinary theta functions but were not actually modular forms.

Ramanujan did not provide proofs for most of his claims about mock theta functions. He stated results and described the essential properties, trusting that mathematicians would eventually verify and extend his work. This is exactly what happened, though it took decades.

The full significance of mock theta functions was not understood until the early 21st century, when Sander Zwegers (in his 2002 Ph.D. thesis under Don Zagier) showed that Ramanujan's mock theta functions are holomorphic parts of harmonic weak Maass forms. This insight connected Ramanujan's last discovery to deep areas of modern mathematics, including modular forms, algebraic geometry, mathematical physics, and string theory.

### The Lost Notebook

In addition to the work described in his letters, Ramanujan filled many pages with mathematical results during his final year in India. These pages were collected after his death and eventually came to be known as Ramanujan's "lost notebook." The manuscript was rediscovered in 1976 by George Andrews of Pennsylvania State University in the library of Trinity College, Cambridge, among papers that had been left by G.N. Watson.

The lost notebook contains approximately 600 results, many of them related to mock theta functions, q-series, and other topics at the frontier of number theory and analysis. Andrews and Bruce Berndt have devoted extensive study to this manuscript, publishing a multi-volume series (*Ramanujan's Lost Notebook*, Parts I–V) that provides proofs and context for the results.

## The Last Letter to Hardy (January 1920)

Ramanujan's last known letter to Hardy was written in January 1920. It is one of the most remarkable documents in the history of mathematics. In it, Ramanujan introduced mock theta functions and laid out his vision for an entirely new area of mathematical inquiry. The letter begins:

> "I am extremely sorry for not writing you a single letter up to now... I discovered very interesting functions recently which I call 'Mock' ϑ-functions."

He went on to describe the defining properties of these functions and to list 17 specific examples. The letter conveys both the excitement of new discovery and the awareness of limited time — Ramanujan knew he was gravely ill.

Hardy recognized the importance of the letter immediately, though he could not fully verify or understand all the results at the time. He later described the mock theta functions as Ramanujan's most profound contribution.

## Death: April 26, 1920

Srinivasa Ramanujan died on April 26, 1920, at the house on Harrington Road in Chetput, Madras. He was 32 years old.

His wife Janaki and his mother Komalatammal were at his side. According to accounts from those present, Ramanujan was working on mathematics almost until the very end. He left behind notebooks filled with results that mathematicians would study for the rest of the century and beyond.

The cause of death was officially recorded as tuberculosis, though the alternative diagnosis of hepatic amoebiasis remains a credible possibility.

Ramanujan's body was cremated the same day at the cremation ground in Chetput, Madras, in accordance with Hindu Brahmin customs.

## Legacy and Posthumous Recognition

Ramanujan's death at 32 was a devastating loss to mathematics. Hardy later wrote that Ramanujan's death was the greatest blow he had ever received, and that his collaboration with Ramanujan was "the one romantic incident in my life."

### Mathematical Legacy

Ramanujan left behind three major notebooks (the first two compiled in India before going to Cambridge, and the third — the "lost notebook" — compiled in India after his return) containing thousands of results, many of them unproved. The systematic study of these notebooks has occupied generations of mathematicians and continues to yield new insights.

Key areas of mathematics that bear Ramanujan's direct influence include:
- The theory of partitions and q-series
- Mock theta functions and harmonic Maass forms
- Modular forms and modular equations
- Analytic number theory (the circle method)
- The theory of continued fractions
- Elliptic functions and hypergeometric series
- Probabilistic number theory (normal order of arithmetic functions)

### Honors and Memorials

- **Ramanujan Journal**: A mathematical journal founded in 1997, dedicated to areas of mathematics influenced by Ramanujan.
- **SASTRA Ramanujan Prize**: An annual prize awarded to young mathematicians for outstanding contributions to areas influenced by Ramanujan.
- **National Mathematics Day (India)**: December 22 — Ramanujan's birthday — is celebrated as National Mathematics Day in India, declared by Prime Minister Manmohan Singh in 2012.
- **Postage stamps**: India has issued multiple postage stamps commemorating Ramanujan (1962, 2011, 2012).
- **The Man Who Knew Infinity**: Robert Kanigel's 1991 biography, later adapted into a 2015 film starring Dev Patel as Ramanujan and Jeremy Irons as Hardy.
- **Ramanujan Museum**: A museum dedicated to Ramanujan was established in Chennai.
- **Bust at Trinity College**: A bust of Ramanujan was installed at Trinity College, Cambridge.

### Hardy's Reflections

G.H. Hardy wrote extensively about Ramanujan after his death. In his famous lecture and essay "Ramanujan" (published in 1940), Hardy attempted to assess Ramanujan's mathematical abilities on a scale, assigning numerical ratings to various mathematicians. He gave Ramanujan a score of 100 for "natural mathematical ability," alongside Euler and Jacobi, while giving himself only 25 and Littlewood 30. Hardy considered Ramanujan to be in the very highest rank of mathematical genius the world has ever produced.

Hardy also reflected on the tragedy of Ramanujan's limited formal training, wondering what Ramanujan might have achieved had he received a proper mathematical education. But he also acknowledged that Ramanujan's lack of conventional training may have been partly responsible for the extraordinary originality of his work — an originality that conventional training might have constrained.
