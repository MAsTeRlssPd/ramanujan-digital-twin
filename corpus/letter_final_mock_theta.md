# Ramanujan's Last Letter to Hardy: Mock Theta Functions (January 1920)

## Context: A Dying Mathematician's Final Discovery

In January 1920, approximately three months before his death on April 26, 1920, Srinivasa Ramanujan wrote what would be his last known letter to G.H. Hardy. Written from his sickbed in Madras (now Chennai), India, this letter introduced one of the most profound and mysterious mathematical concepts of the 20th century: mock theta functions.

Ramanujan had returned to India from England in March 1919, his health severely compromised. Despite being gravely ill — likely suffering from tuberculosis or hepatic amoebiasis — he continued to work on mathematics with extraordinary intensity. The letter to Hardy was the culmination of this final burst of creative activity.

The letter is remarkable not only for its mathematical content but for its circumstances. Here was a man who knew he was dying, whose body was failing, who chose to spend his dwindling energy describing a new class of mathematical functions that would not be fully understood for another 80 years.

## The Letter's Opening

Ramanujan began the letter with an apology for his long silence:

> "I am extremely sorry for not writing you a single letter up to now. I discovered very interesting functions recently which I call 'Mock' ϑ-functions. Unlike the 'False' ϑ-functions (studied partially by Prof. Rogers in his interesting paper) they enter into mathematics as beautifully as the ordinary theta functions."

This opening immediately establishes several key points: Ramanujan was aware of Rogers's work on false theta functions, he distinguished his new functions from them, and he was confident that his mock theta functions would prove to be mathematically significant.

## What Are Mock Theta Functions?

To understand mock theta functions, one must first understand ordinary theta functions. Classical theta functions, studied extensively by Jacobi and others, are a class of special functions that arise naturally in the theory of elliptic functions, modular forms, and number theory. They satisfy certain transformation properties under the modular group and have well-defined behavior at "cusps" — certain boundary points of the upper half-plane.

A key property of ordinary theta functions and modular forms is their behavior at cusps: they can be expressed as a combination of exponential terms with a specific asymptotic structure.

Ramanujan defined his mock theta functions by describing what they are *not*: they are functions that resemble theta functions in many ways but fail to be true modular forms. Specifically, Ramanujan described a mock theta function as a function f(q) satisfying two conditions:

1. **For every root of unity ζ** (i.e., every cusp), there exists a theta function θ_ζ(q) such that the difference f(q) - θ_ζ(q) is bounded as q → ζ radially from within the unit disk.

2. **There is no single theta function** θ(q) that works for *all* roots of unity simultaneously — that is, no single theta function can be subtracted from f(q) to make the result bounded at every cusp.

This definition captures the essential "mockness" of these functions: they look like theta functions locally (near each cusp) but cannot be globally identified with any theta function.

## The 17 Examples

Ramanujan provided 17 specific examples of mock theta functions in his letter, organized by their "order." The order of a mock theta function is related to the level of the associated modular structure.

### Order 3 Mock Theta Functions (4 functions)

Ramanujan listed four mock theta functions of order 3:

**f(q)** = Σ_{n=0}^{∞} q^(n²) / (-q; q)_n²

**φ(q)** = Σ_{n=0}^{∞} q^(n²) / (-q²; q²)_n

**ψ(q)** = Σ_{n=1}^{∞} q^(n²) / (q; q²)_n

**χ(q)** = Σ_{n=0}^{∞} q^(n²) (-q; q)_n / (-q³; q³)_n

where (a; q)_n denotes the q-Pochhammer symbol: (a; q)_n = ∏_{k=0}^{n-1} (1 - aq^k).

These functions involve q-series with squared exponents and products in the denominator. They have deep connections to partition theory: for instance, the coefficients of f(q) are related to the number of partitions of integers with certain specific properties.

### Order 5 Mock Theta Functions (10 functions)

Ramanujan listed ten mock theta functions of order 5, which he divided into two groups. These are more complex than the order 3 functions and involve more intricate q-series structures. Among them are functions denoted f₀(q), f₁(q), φ₀(q), φ₁(q), ψ₀(q), ψ₁(q), χ₀(q), χ₁(q), F₀(q), and F₁(q).

For example:

**f₀(q)** = Σ_{n=0}^{∞} q^(n²) / (q; q)_n

**f₁(q)** = Σ_{n=0}^{∞} q^(n(n+1)) / (q; q)_n

Ramanujan stated several identities relating these functions to each other and to classical theta functions. He also stated asymptotic formulas for their behavior near roots of unity.

### Order 7 Mock Theta Functions (3 functions)

Ramanujan listed three mock theta functions of order 7. These are the most mysterious of his examples and were the least understood for decades after his death. The order 7 functions involve even more complex q-series and their modular properties were the last to be fully explained.

### Order 10 Mock Theta Functions

Ramanujan also briefly mentioned functions that could be classified as order 10, though his treatment of these was less detailed than the others.

## Ramanujan's Claims and Conjectures

Beyond listing the 17 functions, Ramanujan made several specific claims:

1. **Relations among the functions**: He stated identities connecting different mock theta functions of the same order. For order 5, he gave specific linear relations among the ten functions.

2. **Asymptotic behavior**: He described the precise asymptotic behavior of the mock theta functions as q approaches various roots of unity.

3. **Connection to classical functions**: He noted that mock theta functions share many formal properties with ordinary theta functions — they satisfy similar q-series identities, have related symmetries, and arise naturally in combinatorial contexts.

4. **Euler-type expansions**: He showed that some mock theta functions could be expressed as Eulerian series (infinite products or quotients of q-Pochhammer symbols), connecting them to the theory of partitions.

Ramanujan provided little or no proof for these claims, consistent with his lifelong mathematical style. He trusted that the results were correct and that mathematicians would eventually verify them.

## Hardy's Reaction

Hardy received the letter and immediately recognized its importance, but the content was unlike anything he had seen before. He could verify some of the specific identities but could not fully grasp the overarching framework that Ramanujan was describing. Mock theta functions did not fit neatly into any existing category of mathematical objects.

Hardy later described the mock theta functions as perhaps Ramanujan's most original contribution to mathematics. He wrote about the letter in his 1940 essay on Ramanujan, noting the extraordinary circumstances under which it was produced and the depth of the ideas it contained.

After Ramanujan's death, Hardy entrusted the study of the mock theta functions to G.N. Watson, who published two important papers on the subject: "The Final Problem: An Account of the Mock Theta Functions" (1936) and "The Mock Theta Functions (2)" (1937). Watson verified many of Ramanujan's claims and proved several identities, but the deeper structure remained elusive.

## The Long Road to Understanding

For most of the 20th century, mock theta functions remained enigmatic. Mathematicians could verify individual identities and prove specific results, but there was no satisfying theoretical framework that explained what mock theta functions truly *are* and why they behave as they do.

### Key Developments

- **G.N. Watson (1930s)**: Proved many of Ramanujan's specific claims about order 3 and order 5 mock theta functions.
- **Selberg (1930s-1950s)**: Atle Selberg studied mock theta functions in the context of partition theory and proved several important results.
- **Andrews (1966)**: George Andrews discovered the "lost notebook" and found extensive additional material on mock theta functions beyond what was in the letter to Hardy.
- **Andrews and Garvan (1980s-1990s)**: Developed the theory of "cranks" for partitions, which was connected to mock theta functions.
- **Zwegers (2002)**: The breakthrough came in Sander Zwegers's 2002 Ph.D. thesis, written under Don Zagier at the University of Bonn. Zwegers showed that Ramanujan's mock theta functions are the holomorphic parts of harmonic weak Maass forms.

### Zwegers's Breakthrough

Zwegers's key insight was that each of Ramanujan's mock theta functions can be "completed" by adding a specific non-holomorphic correction term — a period integral of a unary theta function — to obtain a function that transforms like a true modular form (specifically, a harmonic weak Maass form of weight 1/2).

In other words, a mock theta function f(q) is the holomorphic part of a function:

F(τ) = f(q) + non-holomorphic part

where F(τ) satisfies modular transformation properties. The "non-holomorphic part" is what Ramanujan was missing — the piece needed to restore full modularity. But Ramanujan had perceived the essential nature of these functions without this framework, relying on his extraordinary intuition.

### Modern Significance

Since Zwegers's thesis, mock theta functions and mock modular forms have become a major area of mathematical research. They appear in:

- **Number theory**: Partition identities, ranks and cranks of partitions, q-series.
- **Algebraic geometry**: Donaldson invariants and curve-counting problems.
- **Mathematical physics**: Black hole entropy calculations in string theory (work by Dabholkar, Murthy, Zagier, and others).
- **Combinatorics**: Generating functions for various combinatorial objects.
- **Representation theory**: Characters of certain infinite-dimensional Lie algebras.

The development of the theory of mock modular forms — triggered by Ramanujan's deathbed letter — is one of the most active areas of modern number theory. Kathrin Bringmann, Ken Ono, and many others have made fundamental contributions building on this foundation.

## The Letter as a Testament

Ramanujan's last letter to Hardy is more than a mathematical document. It is a testament to the power of mathematical intuition and the human drive to create, even in the face of death. Ramanujan, with no formal training in modern analysis and no knowledge of the theory of modular forms as it would develop in the 20th century, perceived the existence of a new class of mathematical objects that would take the world's best mathematicians 80 years to fully understand.

As Ken Ono has written, Ramanujan's mock theta functions were "a deathbed gift to mathematics" — a final act of genius that continues to yield new insights more than a century later.
