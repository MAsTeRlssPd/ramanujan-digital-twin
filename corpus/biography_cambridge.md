# Srinivasa Ramanujan: The Cambridge Years (1913–1919)

## The Letter That Changed Everything (January 1913)

On January 16, 1913, Srinivasa Ramanujan, a 25-year-old clerk at the Madras Port Trust earning roughly 25 rupees per month, composed a letter to G.H. Hardy, a Fellow of Trinity College and one of the leading pure mathematicians in England. The letter was approximately ten pages long and contained around 120 mathematical formulas and results, presented largely without proof.

Ramanujan had previously written to two other English mathematicians — H.F. Baker and E.W. Hobson — but received no substantive response. Hardy was his third attempt. The letter began with a now-famous self-introduction: Ramanujan described himself as a clerk in the Accounts Department of the Port Trust, with no university education, and stated that he had been conducting mathematical research on his own.

Hardy received the letter on or around January 16, 1913. His initial reaction was skepticism — he suspected a possible fraud. But as he examined the enclosed results more carefully, many of which he could not prove or disprove, he became increasingly astonished. He consulted his colleague and collaborator J.E. Littlewood that same evening. After several hours of study, Hardy and Littlewood concluded that the letter was the work of a mathematician "of the highest class" — as Hardy later put it, the results "must be true, because if they were not true, no one would have had the imagination to invent them."

Hardy wrote back to Ramanujan, asking for proofs of some results and expressing keen interest. This exchange initiated a correspondence that would last for years and ultimately bring Ramanujan to England.

## Journey to England (1914)

Bringing Ramanujan to Cambridge was not straightforward. There were significant obstacles, both practical and cultural. Ramanujan was an orthodox Tamil Brahmin, and crossing the ocean ("kalapani") was traditionally considered a violation of caste rules. His mother, Komalatammal, initially objected to the journey.

According to family tradition, the goddess Namagiri appeared to Komalatammal in a dream and gave her blessing for Ramanujan to travel. After this, her opposition subsided. Ramanujan himself spent three days in prayer at the Namagiri temple in Namakkal before departing.

Financial support for the journey was arranged through the efforts of several individuals, including Sir Francis Spring (Chairman of the Madras Port Trust), R. Ramachandra Rao, and the University of Madras, which awarded Ramanujan a research scholarship of £250 per year.

Ramanujan departed from Madras on March 17, 1914, aboard the S.S. Nevasa. He arrived in London on April 14, 1914. He was met by E.H. Neville, a Fellow of Trinity College whom Hardy had sent to Madras earlier that year to help facilitate Ramanujan's travel.

## Life at Trinity College

Ramanujan was admitted to Trinity College, Cambridge, one of the most prestigious academic institutions in the world. He was given rooms in college and had access to the university's libraries and mathematical community.

However, life at Cambridge was profoundly difficult for Ramanujan in many non-mathematical ways. He was a strict vegetarian in a country where vegetarian food — especially of the South Indian variety — was extremely difficult to obtain. During World War I (1914–1918), food shortages made this even worse. Ramanujan often cooked for himself, but obtaining suitable ingredients was a constant struggle. He sometimes went without proper meals.

The English climate was another hardship. Ramanujan had spent his entire life in tropical South India, and the cold, damp winters of Cambridge took a severe toll on his health and spirits. He had no experience with cold weather and found it physically oppressive.

Socially, Ramanujan was isolated. He was the only Indian student in the mathematical community at Cambridge, and cultural differences were vast. While Hardy and a few others treated him with respect and collegiality, the broader social environment could be unwelcoming. Ramanujan was deeply homesick and missed his family, his wife Janaki, and the familiar rhythms of life in South India.

World War I further compounded his isolation. Many students and fellows had left Cambridge for military service, and the usual intellectual and social life of the university was greatly diminished. Littlewood himself was away doing war-related ballistics work for much of the war.

## Mathematical Collaboration with Hardy

Despite the personal difficulties, Ramanujan's mathematical output at Cambridge was extraordinary. His collaboration with G.H. Hardy was one of the most remarkable partnerships in the history of mathematics.

Hardy provided something Ramanujan had never before had: a world-class mathematical interlocutor who could understand, challenge, and refine his results. Hardy also brought the rigor of the European mathematical tradition — careful proofs, precise definitions, and logical structure — which complemented Ramanujan's extraordinary intuition and ability to perceive deep patterns.

Their working styles were very different. Ramanujan typically arrived at results through intuition, experimentation with numerical examples, and processes he sometimes described as receiving formulas in visions or dreams. Hardy, by contrast, was a product of the rigorous Cambridge mathematical tradition and insisted on formal proof. Hardy later said that trying to teach Ramanujan formal proofs was a delicate matter: he did not want to destroy Ramanujan's intuitive genius by forcing him into conventional channels, but he also knew that without proofs, many of Ramanujan's results would not be accepted by the mathematical community.

### Key Collaborative Works

Among the most important works produced during the Cambridge period were:

1. **Highly Composite Numbers (1915)**: Ramanujan's paper on highly composite numbers — positive integers with more divisors than any smaller positive integer — was published in the *Proceedings of the London Mathematical Society*. This was Ramanujan's longest paper, and it was based on work he had begun in India. The published version was actually an abridged version; the full manuscript was much longer.

2. **The Hardy-Ramanujan Asymptotic Formula for Partitions (1918)**: This is perhaps the most celebrated result of the Hardy-Ramanujan collaboration. The partition function p(n) counts the number of ways a positive integer n can be expressed as a sum of positive integers (where order does not matter). Hardy and Ramanujan developed a powerful asymptotic formula for p(n) using the "circle method," a technique involving complex analysis and modular functions. The formula gives:

   p(n) ~ (1 / (4n√3)) * exp(π * √(2n/3))

   as n → ∞. This result was groundbreaking in analytic number theory and the circle method became one of the most important tools in the field. It was later refined by Hans Rademacher into an exact convergent series.

3. **Normal Order of Prime Factors (1917)**: Hardy and Ramanujan proved that the number of distinct prime factors of a typical integer n is approximately log(log(n)). This was a foundational result in probabilistic number theory and introduced the concept of the "normal order" of an arithmetic function.

4. **Ramanujan's Tau Function and Conjectures**: Ramanujan studied the coefficients τ(n) of the discriminant modular form Δ(z) and made several deep conjectures about their properties, including multiplicativity and bounds on their size. The conjecture on bounds (|τ(p)| ≤ 2p^(11/2)) was finally proved by Pierre Deligne in 1973 as a consequence of his proof of the Weil conjectures.

## Academic Honors

### B.A. by Research (1916)

In 1916, Ramanujan was awarded a Bachelor of Arts degree by research (later redesignated as a Ph.D.) from Cambridge, based on his work on highly composite numbers. This was his first formal academic qualification beyond high school.

### Fellow of the Royal Society (1918)

On February 28, 1918, Ramanujan was elected a Fellow of the Royal Society (FRS), one of the highest honors in British science. He was one of the youngest Fellows ever elected at the time, at age 30. He was only the second Indian to receive this honor (after Ardaseer Cursetjee in 1841). His election was proposed by Hardy and supported by other prominent mathematicians.

The citation for his election noted his investigations in elliptic functions, the theory of numbers, and the theory of continued fractions.

### Fellow of Trinity College (1918)

On October 13, 1918, Ramanujan was elected a Fellow of Trinity College, Cambridge. He was the first Indian to be elected a Fellow of Trinity. This was a deeply significant honor, as Trinity was one of the most prestigious colleges in the world, with a fellowship list that included Isaac Newton among its historical members.

Hardy had campaigned strenuously for Ramanujan's election, and there is evidence that an earlier attempt in 1917 had been unsuccessful, possibly due in part to racial prejudice among some fellows. The successful 1918 election came only after Ramanujan's FRS had been secured, which made opposition more difficult.

## Published Papers

During his time in England, Ramanujan published approximately 21 papers, either solely or in collaboration with Hardy. These papers appeared in leading journals including the *Proceedings of the London Mathematical Society*, the *Quarterly Journal of Mathematics*, the *Transactions of the Cambridge Philosophical Society*, and the *Proceedings of the Cambridge Philosophical Society*.

His papers covered an astonishing range of topics: highly composite numbers, partition functions, the tau function, representations of integers as sums of squares, modular equations, definite integrals, hypergeometric series, continued fractions, and properties of Bernoulli numbers.

## The Toll of Cambridge

Despite his mathematical triumphs, the Cambridge years exacted a devastating physical toll on Ramanujan. By 1917, his health had begun to seriously deteriorate, and he would spend much of his remaining time in England in and out of sanatoria. The combination of poor nutrition, the cold climate, wartime deprivations, overwork, and possible pre-existing conditions created a medical crisis that would ultimately cut short one of the most remarkable mathematical careers in history.

Hardy later reflected on the Cambridge years with a mixture of pride and regret. He was proud of what he and Ramanujan had accomplished together, but he wondered whether bringing Ramanujan to England had been the right decision — whether the mathematical gains had been worth the cost to Ramanujan's health and, ultimately, his life.
