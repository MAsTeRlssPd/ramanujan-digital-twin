# Mock Theta Functions

Mock theta functions represent the crowning achievement of Ramanujan's mathematical life. He discovered them in the final months of his life after returning to India in 1919, while suffering from a fatal illness.

## The Final Letter to Hardy

In January 1920, Ramanujan wrote his last letter to Hardy, introducing a completely new class of functions:

> "I am extremely sorry for not writing you a single letter up to now... I discovered very interesting functions recently which I call 'Mock' θ-functions. Unlike the 'False' θ-functions (studied partially by Prof. Rogers in his interesting paper), they enter into mathematics as beautifully as the ordinary theta functions."

He listed 17 examples of these mock theta functions, categorizing them by "order" (third, fifth, and seventh order). For example, one of his third-order mock theta functions is defined as:

$$f(q) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{q^{n^2}}{(1+q)^2(1+q^2)^2\cdots(1+q^n)^2}$$

## Significance

Classical theta functions possess beautiful transformation properties under the modular group (they are modular forms). Ramanujan's mock theta functions look like classical theta functions in their $q$-series expansions, and they have similar asymptotic behavior at the roots of unity (the cusps), but they are *not* modular forms.

For nearly 80 years, mock theta functions remained mysterious. Mathematicians could prove individual identities, but the overarching theory explaining what they actually *were* eluded everyone.

It wasn't until 2002 that Sander Zwegers, in his Ph.D. thesis, finally provided the theoretical framework: mock theta functions are the holomorphic parts of **harmonic weak Maass forms**. This breakthrough integrated Ramanujan's dying discoveries into the mainstream of modern modular forms theory, proving once again his unparalleled intuition.
