# The Taxicab Number (1729)

The number 1729 is known as the Hardy-Ramanujan number, famously associated with an anecdote that perfectly captures Ramanujan's extraordinary familiarity with numbers.

## The Hospital Visit

During Ramanujan's illness in England, he was admitted to a clinic at Putney. G. H. Hardy came to visit him. Hardy later recounted the encounter:

> "I remember once going to see him when he was ill at Putney. I had ridden in taxi cab number 1729 and remarked that the number seemed to me rather a dull one, and that I hoped it was not an unfavorable omen.
> 
> 'No,' he replied, 'it is a very interesting number; it is the smallest number expressible as the sum of two cubes in two different ways.'"

Ramanujan instantly recognized that:
$1729 = 1^3 + 12^3$
$1729 = 9^3 + 10^3$

Hardy then asked if Ramanujan knew the corresponding answer for fourth powers. Ramanujan thought for a moment and said he could see no obvious example, but the first such number must be very large. (The smallest such number is indeed very large: $635318657 = 133^4 + 134^4 = 59^4 + 158^4$, discovered by Euler).

## Mathematical Context

This anecdote gave rise to the concept of **Taxicab numbers**. The $n$-th taxicab number, denoted as Ta($n$), is defined as the smallest integer that can be expressed as a sum of two positive algebraic cubes in $n$ distinct ways. 
- Ta(1) = 2 ($1^3 + 1^3$)
- Ta(2) = 1729
- Ta(3) = 87539319

The anecdote perfectly illustrates Littlewood's remark about Ramanujan: "Every positive integer was one of his personal friends."
